Phase Zero

Mindset Shifts

Before using these tools effectively, you must understand what they actually are—not what you imagine them to be.

What LLMs Actually Are

An AI language model predicts the next word based on the words previously written and spoken by people. That's it. That's the whole function.

Its basic nature is to produce word sequences that match the patterns it learned during training. Every LLM is trained on different sets of data—some bigger, some smaller, some with better quality. For simplicity: think of the training dataset as the Internet.

Because of this basic nature:

  • It doesn't understand what it's writing
  • It doesn't reason
  • It doesn't have goals

Human mental work is very different. We use our minds to change our situation and keep developing. We understand what we write. We reason. We have goals.

The Multiplication Table Analogy

Think about multiplication tables—maybe the first pattern-completion tool we all learned.

"Seven eights are fifty-six." You memorize this sequence of words. It gives you the right answer, but you don't need to understand why. You just need the pattern.

The comparison is useful. Both multiplication tables and AI tools take mental work that used to be done individually and make it instantly available to everyone.

The crucial difference

7×8=56 is always true—it follows from how numbers work. LLMs give you probable patterns based on the Internet.

We trust multiplication tables completely because they're always right. We check AI outputs because they're based on probabilities, not certainties.

The Dishwasher Analogy

When explaining these tools to colleagues who don't use them, the dishwasher analogy helps.

A dishwasher doesn't copy hand-washing motions—it cleans dishes through a completely different method. Hot water circulation instead of scrubbing. This different method has different trade-offs:

Dimension Hand Washing Dishwasher
Time type Sequential (your attention) Parallel (runs while you sleep)
Visible progress Clean immediately Dirty pile visible first

The same pattern applies to AI tools. They don't copy your thinking process—they produce outputs through probability distributions. The method is different; the trade-offs are different.

Colleagues evaluating the tool by "does it think like I do?" miss the point. The question is: does it produce good enough results through its method?

The Line: Abundant vs. Scarce Work

First: AI tools make certain mental work abundant. Work that relies on typical patterns, on probable patterns, becomes abundant. This follows directly from what the tool is.

Second: When work becomes abundant, its value drops. This is basic supply and demand. When something scarce becomes abundant, and people need about the same amount of it, the price falls. Nobody decides this—it just happens.

Third: Human work moves toward whatever is still scarce. People need to survive and grow. If abundant work pays less and scarce work pays more, people naturally focus where scarcity remains. This isn't strategy—it's cause and effect.

Fourth: This line keeps moving. As AI tools improve, more types of work cross from scarce to abundant. The line where human work concentrates shifts.

Where the Line Is Right Now

There's a practical way to tell when work has become abundant: the one-prompt test.

When you can do something with just one prompt, it's crossed into abundant territory. Anyone with the tool can do it. It needs no special knowledge to guide it, no back-and-forth to refine it, no expertise to check if it's good.

The line sits just past this point—where pattern-completion starts failing.

Why People Push Back

When someone has spent years building a specific mental skill—legal analysis, software design, medical diagnosis—and that skill suddenly becomes abundant, their way of making a living is genuinely at risk.

The pushback isn't irrational. It's accurate recognition that their situation has changed. Fear follows from real danger.

Material conditions are the real causes. Economic security lets you experiment without risking your survival. Extra time and energy let you learn. Job security lets you fail without disaster.

These aren't character traits—they're conditions that determine what's possible.

Three Patterns of Response

Pattern Behavior Follows From
A: Reliable Use Use tools only where outcomes are reliable from single prompts Understanding that these tasks are abundant, plus limited time to invest
B: Minimal/No Use Avoid tools entirely or use minimally Not understanding the tools, or conditions that don't allow learning investment
C: Edge Investment Invest time learning where tools help and where they fail Understanding pattern-completion, having conditions that allow experimentation

None of these is morally better. Each follows from conditions.

The Manager Mindset

Agentic CLIs can work for minutes or hours on complex tasks. The natural instinct is to watch—the streaming output is compelling, and curiosity about the process is real.

Resist this instinct.

Once you've given clear instructions and started autonomous execution, move on. Prepare the next briefing document. Work on something else. Check results when the LLM signals completion.

This shift from "operator watching a tool" to "manager delegating work" takes practice. The productivity gains require it.

The manager mindset also applies to review. You don't evaluate process; you evaluate output. Did it produce what you asked for? Does the result work? Those questions matter. How it got there doesn't.

Confidence Builds on Itself

A pattern emerged across course participants: confidence compounds.

Early attempts feel uncertain. "Will this work? Am I doing it right? What if I break something?" The uncertainty slows everything down.

Successful completions build confidence. Confidence enables faster attempts. Faster attempts produce more completions. The cycle accelerates.

Key Insight

"Once you get ahead of the technology, you don't lose that advantage." The learning curve is steep, but the plateau is high.

When It Fails, That's Information

When attempts fail—when the output is unusable, when iteration doesn't improve it, when the time spent beats just doing it yourself—that failure tells you something.

It marks where the line currently sits. The task you tried still needs scarce human ability. Pattern-completion can't handle it reliably yet.

This isn't about you being inadequate. It's data about the current state of the tool relative to your specific task.

The line moves. A task that fails today might work in three months, six months, a year. Failures map the territory.

Phase One

Briefing

Define what you're solving without assuming how.

Purpose

This phase happens in the Web UI (Claude.ai, ChatGPT, or Gemini web). The interface matters: questions get answered methodically, prompts get edited before submission, each question gets processed carefully. This is where judgment happens—where you figure out what you actually want.

The Process

  1. Open a web-based chatbot
  2. Describe your goal at a high level
  3. Let the LLM ask you questions for 20-40 minutes
  4. Answer each question carefully—each answer becomes an instruction
  5. Review the briefing document it produces
  6. Verify it captured what you actually requested

Why 40 minutes on requirements matters

This feels slow. No coding yet. Nothing visible happening. But this is where the actual work occurs.

The LLM asks: What problem does this solve? What are the essential features? What can wait for version two? How should the interface work? What data needs to be stored?

Each question refines what will actually get built. Each answer becomes an instruction that the coding LLM will follow literally.

These are specifications for an agent that won't be able to ask follow-up questions.

What to Capture

  • Problem: What problem are we solving?
  • User: For whom? What's their context, skill level, situation?
  • Constraints: What limits exist? Time, budget, technology, regulations?
  • Success criteria: What does "done" look like?
  • Scope boundaries: What's in version 1? What can wait?

What to Deliberately Exclude

  • Technology choices
  • Architecture decisions
  • Implementation details
  • Specific tools or frameworks

Critical Rule

Deliberately leave the "how" blank. Don't let the LLM make technical assumptions at this stage.

The reason: if you combine problem definition with technical feasibility in one step, the LLM fills knowledge gaps with plausible-sounding fiction. Separating them prevents this.

Document Length Guidelines

Context Target Length Rationale
Solo project 5-10 pages acceptable Only you need to read it
Business person in team 2-4 pages Must be digestible by all members
Team alignment 1 page assumptions + 2-3 page brief Everyone reads, nobody skips

The Team Math Problem

Five people. Twenty pages each. One hundred pages total. Nobody reads a hundred pages. The documents become artifacts that exist but don't function.

2

Phase Two

Research & Validation

Before committing to any approach, verify what's actually possible. This phase prevents the hallucination trap: detailed plans built on fictional capabilities.

The Hallucination Problem

During the course, briefing document development for one project had the LLM confidently describe API capabilities that don't exist. Endpoints that aren't real. Features that were never implemented. The hallucinations were detailed and plausible.

The resulting briefing document specified an architecture that couldn't be built. Hours of work toward something impossible.

Root cause

LLMs predict a world that should logically exist, not what actually exists.

Examples of Probable but False Assumptions

Consider these "probable" but false assumptions discovered during the course:

  • Adobe Analytics "should" have an MCP server—it's a major analytics platform. It doesn't.
  • QuickBooks "should" have a modern REST API—it's 2025. Check the actual documentation.
  • Looker "should" pull data in real-time. It can't—need to export to BigQuery first.
  • YouTube "should" let you download comments via API. Not how it works.
  • GA4 "should" handle unlimited concurrent API requests. It has rate limits that break with multiple users.
  • Google Ads MCP "should" be officially maintained. It's community-maintained.

All of these are probable patterns. An LLM writing about these systems will confidently describe them. None are true.

The Failure Mode Is Worse Than Wrong Answers

When Claude Code hits a wall during implementation—when the API endpoint doesn't exist, when the feature isn't supported—it doesn't stop. It creates fake data to continue. Mock responses. Placeholder values.

The system appears to work. It runs without errors. But it's running on fabricated data.

The architecture was solid. The code was clean. The data was fictional.

The Research Process

Step 1: Identify Assumptions

Ask: "What are the top 10 assumptions this plan makes about external systems?"

For each assumption about an external API, service, or capability:

  • Does this API endpoint exist?
  • Does this feature work as described?
  • What are the rate limits?
  • Is this officially supported or community-maintained?
  • What are the actual capabilities vs. what "should" exist?

Step 2: Verify Each Assumption

  • Check real documentation, not LLM memory
  • Use web search to find official sources
  • Ask the LLM to cite sources you can verify
  • Test assumptions against actual documentation
  • Look for changelogs, deprecation notices, community issues

Step 3: Document Confirmed Capabilities

Format: "We confirmed [capability] exists via [source]. Limitations: [specific limits found]."

Step 4: Flag Unverifiable Assumptions

If you can't verify something, explicitly mark it as unconfirmed. Don't proceed as if it's true.

Time Investment

Ten minutes of research can prevent hours of building on false foundations.

Phase Three

Architecture

Build only on confirmed ground. Now—and only now—decide on structure.

Technology Justification

Architecture documents frequently list technology choices without explaining why.

"We'll use FastAPI." Why FastAPI? "We'll store data in BigQuery." Why BigQuery?

These documents answer "could this work?" but not "why is this the best choice?"

The difference matters. When you can't explain why you chose something, you don't actually know if it's right.

❌ Not this

"The system will use FastAPI for the backend layer."

✓ This

"FastAPI handles the backend. We chose it over Flask because we need async support for concurrent MCP calls, and over Express because the team has Python experience."

Two sentences. Covers the reasoning. Makes the choice defensible.

The Annotation Technique

Define a mini-language at the start of your editing session:

Tag Meaning
[delete] Remove this section entirely
[?] I don't understand this, needs clarification
[wrong] This contradicts what I know
→ Change this to something else

Mark up the document. Paste the annotated version back to Claude with "Here's my feedback on your draft. Address each annotation."

The goal: knowing every word in your final document. Not because you wrote each word, but because you approved each word through deliberate review.

The Two-Tier Document Structure

A tension emerged: 3 pages isn't enough for serious implementation, but 20 pages × 5 people doesn't work for collaboration.

The solution is layers:

Tier 1: Assumptions Document (1 page)

A short list of key decisions the architecture depends on. Not explanations—just statements.

Examples:

  • "One MCP server per data source"
  • "No cross-platform queries in v1"
  • "Security handled by containerization, not code"
  • "All data stays local, no cloud storage"

The purpose is alignment. The framing—"assumptions" rather than "decisions"—invites challenge.

Tier 2: Brief (2-3 pages, primarily business owner)

Problem definition. Who it's for. What success looks like. Constraints. No technical implementation details.

The brief is 80% the business person's document. Technical people read it to understand what problem they're solving.

Tier 3: Architecture (3-7 pages, primarily technical owner)

How the system works. Technology choices with justifications. Component relationships. Data flows.

The architecture is 80% the technical person's document. Business people read it to verify their requirements are addressed.

Tier 4: Deep Technical (as needed)

Implementation details. Code structure. Configuration specifics. API schemas.

This tier exists for reference during implementation. It's not shared reading for the team—it's working documentation for whoever builds it.

The workflow: Everyone reads Tier 1 (assumptions) and Tier 2 (brief). Technical members read Tier 3 (architecture). Tier 4 is reference material.

Phase Four

Execution

The three-interface pattern: Web UI for requirements, CLI for execution, testing environment for iteration.

The Three Interfaces

This division isn't arbitrary—it follows from what each interface does well.

Interface Purpose When to Use
Web UI Requirements gathering, judgment, questions Phase 1: Briefing
Claude Code (CLI) Autonomous execution, file creation, code Phase 4: Building
Testing Environment Identify problems, describe fixes Phase 5: Iteration

The Briefing Document Handoff

The briefing document becomes critical. It's the last chance for judgment to shape what gets built. Once Claude Code starts, it follows those instructions without anyone there to course-correct.

Think of this document as...

What you'd write for a developer who starts work tomorrow. They won't be able to ask questions while they work. Everything they need must be in writing.

Before handoff:

  • Read the briefing carefully
  • Verify Claude understood correctly
  • Check for misunderstandings
  • Confirm nothing is missing

This is the last checkpoint. After this, execution begins.

Permission Modes

Default Mode (Ask before each action)

Should I create this file? Should I run this command? Should I install this package?

This makes sense for learning. Each step becomes visible, understandable, controllable.

Bypass Mode ("dangerously skip permissions")

In this mode, Claude Code doesn't ask. It just executes. Fully autonomous.

Give Claude Code instructions and walk away. Come back to a finished project.

The Trade-off

Pure autonomous approach isn't what you'd use for real work. It's too risky. You can't evaluate what you can't understand.

Better pattern: Stay involved. Watch what Claude Code does. Toggle between modes based on whether you need speed or oversight.

What Happens During Autonomous Execution

When the briefing gets handed to Claude Code and autonomous mode is enabled:

Claude Code is creating folders, writing frontend code, writing backend code, setting up databases, installing dependencies, starting servers. Without supervision. No questions asked, no confirmations requested, no human judgment involved.

This is the shift. Not "AI helps you code faster." But "AI codes while you're not there."

When you return, there's a working application. Not perfect—bugs exist, some features don't work, some data is placeholder—but it runs.

Time Distribution Example

From the course video demonstration (habit tracker app):

Activity Time Type
Answering questions (briefing) 40 min Active
Reading the briefing 5 min Active
First build (autonomous) 15 min Autonomous
Testing 10 min Active
Second iteration (autonomous) 15 min Autonomous
Final review 5 min Active
Total 90 min

Active participation: ~60 min

Autonomous execution: ~30 min

Phase Five

Testing & Iteration

The first version establishes the foundation. Testing identifies fixes. This is expected, not failure.

Expected: First Version Has Problems

Problems found in course projects:

  • Mobile UI rendering issues
  • Statistics display not developed
  • Placeholder data instead of real data
  • Features that don't work as specified

The first version shows what's possible. Testing reveals what to fix.

The Testing Process

  1. Test the output — Use it. Click things. Enter data. Try edge cases.
  2. Identify problems — What's broken? What's missing? What's wrong?
  3. Describe to Claude Code — Tell it what's broken. You don't debug; you describe. (Can include screenshots—Claude Code can "see" images)
  4. Autonomous fix cycle — Claude Code debugs and fixes autonomously.
  5. Test fixes — Check if the fix worked. Identify remaining problems.
  6. Repeat — Build-test-feedback continues until outputs work.

Your Role in Testing

You don't debug problems. You tell Claude Code what's broken. It debugs and fixes autonomously.

What you do

  • Use the system
  • Notice problems
  • Describe problems clearly
  • Verify fixes

What Claude Code does

  • Diagnose causes
  • Write fixes
  • Run tests
  • Implement changes

Essential

Time Management

LLM work doesn't follow predictable time-to-result ratios. Without guardrails, you can lose entire days to conversations that go nowhere.

The Problem

Traditional work has predictable time-to-result ratios. Cooking dinner takes roughly an hour. A presentation takes roughly a day.

LLM work doesn't follow this pattern.

You can accomplish in one hour what would normally take a week. You can also spend three hours and produce nothing usable. The variance is enormous.

Rule 1: Match the Traditional Time

Before starting, estimate how long the task would take without LLM assistance. Give the LLM attempt that same amount of time.

Task Traditional Time LLM Time Budget
Writing an email 20 minutes 20 minutes
Building a presentation 3 hours 3 hours
Creating a report 1 day 1 day

If it's not working by then, stop. You still have time to do it yourself.

This rule ensures you can't lose more than 2× the normal time on any task.

Rule 2: The 2× Boundary

Start LLM work only when you have double the time remaining before the deadline.

Deadline Normal Time Start LLM By
Friday 3 hours Wednesday (6 hours out)
Tomorrow noon 2 hours Today (4 hours out)
End of day 1 hour 2 hours before deadline

This protects against: LLM attempts that fail, provider outages, rate limits, unexpected complexity.

M

Frameworks

Mental Models

Frameworks for thinking about these tools that emerged from practice.

The Chat as Book

Treat the conversation as reference material, not ephemeral exchange.

  • Copy important outputs to external documents
  • Build a searchable record of what was decided
  • Return to previous conversations for context
  • Don't rely on memory—yours or the LLM's

The Golden Retriever Problem

LLMs are eager to please. They'll attempt tasks they can't do well. They'll confirm assumptions that seem plausible. They won't push back on bad ideas.

Implication: You must provide the judgment. Don't mistake enthusiasm for capability.

The 20-Hour Pattern

Initial attempts typically take about as long as doing the work yourself. You're learning to shape conditions—what prompts work, what iteration patterns help, when to stop and check.

After roughly 20 hours of this investment, the same work that took as long initially now finishes in a fraction of the time.

The categorical shift

Tasks you couldn't attempt at all before—writing Python code, for instance—become possible. Not at expert level, but good enough for your purposes with checking.

From "I can't attempt this without years of training" to "I can attempt this with tool plus limited checking knowledge."

The Writing & Reading Problem

Natural tendency (4 in 5 people)

  • Chat rapidly with the LLM
  • Skim the output quickly
  • Iterate through conversation

Feels productive but often isn't.

What works better

  • Write more slowly and deliberately
  • Read outputs carefully and completely
  • Edit in a text editor before sending
